Looking back at the video of my Round 1 speech, I can barely recognize my own delivery. In Round 1, I stood nearly motionless at the podium, spoke in a close-to-monotone voice, and made eye contact almost exclusively with the middle row of seats directly in front of me. By Round 3, I was moving deliberately away from the podium during key moments, varying my pace and volume intentionally to signal emphasis, and scanning all three sections of the room in rotation. The change is not subtle. The person in that Round 1 recording seems not to have understood yet that public speaking is a performance — that the audience is not just receiving information, they are having an experience, and the speaker is responsible for the quality of that experience.

The skill I am most proud of developing is physical presence — specifically, learning to use the space of the room instead of retreating to the safety of the podium. This took me the longest to get comfortable with because it felt artificial at first. Moving while speaking seemed like a trick rather than a real technique. What changed my mind was the feedback after Round 2, where three different people wrote that my movement in the second section of the speech made the argument feel more urgent. Once I understood that movement was serving the content and not distracting from it, I was able to practice it intentionally rather than fighting it. By Round 3 I was using position changes as deliberate transitions — moving left for the first main point, returning to center for the counterargument, and moving right for the call to action. That structure made the speech feel staged in the best sense.

What I will continue working on is listening as a speaker — staying genuinely present with the audience's reaction rather than just delivering my planned content. I noticed during Round 3 that when one section of the audience responded visibly to a moment, I sped up rather than pausing to let it land. A better speaker would hold the pause and let the response breathe. I want to develop the confidence to do that, which requires trusting the material enough to stop managing it and just be in the room.
