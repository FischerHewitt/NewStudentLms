In the macromolecule identification lab, we used three indicator tests — iodine, Benedict's solution, and Biuret reagent — to detect the presence of different macromolecules in several unknown substances. Iodine turns dark blue or black in the presence of starch, a polysaccharide. Benedict's solution turns orange or brick red when heated with reducing sugars such as glucose or fructose. Biuret reagent turns purple in the presence of proteins. We tested each substance with all three indicators and recorded whether each reaction was positive or negative.

Our results showed that Substance A tested positive for starch (iodine turned dark blue) and negative for both protein and reducing sugars. This indicates that Substance A is composed primarily of a complex carbohydrate — likely a starchy food such as a potato or bread. Substance B was positive for reducing sugars (Benedict's turned orange) and negative for the other two, which suggests it contains simple sugars, consistent with something like fruit juice or a sugar solution. Substance C gave a strong positive Biuret reaction, turning deep purple, while testing negative for starch and sugars — this indicates a high protein content, as we would expect from something like egg white or a protein solution. The pattern of indicator reactions allowed us to draw conclusions about each substance's macromolecule composition without knowing in advance what they were. This is significant because it demonstrates that indicator tests can function as a diagnostic tool — by testing for the presence or absence of specific molecular structures, we can identify what category of macromolecule is dominant in an unknown sample.
