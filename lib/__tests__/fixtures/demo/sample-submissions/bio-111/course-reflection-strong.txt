The concept from Unit 1 that surprised me most was the idea that all biological processes ultimately come down to chemistry — specifically how ATP works as a universal energy currency. Before this class I understood vaguely that cells need energy to survive, but I had never thought about the mechanism. Learning that ATP is hydrolyzed to ADP and releases energy that directly powers cellular work made biology feel much more precise and less mysterious. What surprised me was how universal it is — the same molecule drives a muscle contraction, moves ions across a membrane, and synthesizes proteins. That level of molecular consistency across all living things is remarkable. I think about it now when I exercise, knowing that every contraction is literally running on ATP that my cells regenerated from food I ate.

In Unit 2, the concept that stuck with me most was semi-conservative DNA replication. When I first heard the term I expected a complicated exception to some rule, but the logic behind it is actually elegant: each new DNA double helix retains one original strand as a template, ensuring that copying errors are minimized and that the original sequence is preserved as a reference. What I found personally relevant was the accuracy — roughly one error per billion base pairs before repair. As someone who makes typos writing a single paragraph, the idea that cells copy billions of nucleotides with that level of fidelity, using molecular machinery that physically proofreads each base, felt genuinely impressive.

The Unit 3 concept that resonated with me was natural selection acting on existing variation rather than "aiming" at anything. I had always assumed evolution was directional — that organisms were trying to improve. Learning that natural selection is simply differential reproduction based on traits that happen to improve survival in a specific environment changed how I think about biology and about competition more generally. The Hardy-Weinberg simulation in lab made this concrete: allele frequencies drifted when we violated the equilibrium assumptions, which showed that selection and drift are always operating even when we cannot see them directly.
