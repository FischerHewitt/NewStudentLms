The scientific method is a systematic process scientists use to investigate questions and test explanations. It generally follows these steps: (1) Observation — noticing something in the natural world and asking a question about it. For example, a scientist might observe that plants near a window grow faster than those in a darker corner of a room. (2) Hypothesis — forming a testable prediction. The hypothesis might be: "Plants exposed to more light will grow taller because light provides energy for photosynthesis." (3) Experiment — designing a controlled test. The scientist would grow identical plants under different light conditions, keeping all other variables (soil, water, temperature) the same, so the only difference is light exposure. (4) Data collection and analysis — measuring plant height over several weeks and recording the results. (5) Conclusion — deciding whether the data supports or contradicts the hypothesis. If plants in bright light grew significantly taller, the hypothesis is supported. If not, the experiment might be redesigned or the hypothesis revised.

Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells are the two fundamental categories of cells. The key difference is that eukaryotic cells have a true membrane-bound nucleus containing their DNA, while prokaryotic cells have no nucleus — their DNA floats freely in the cytoplasm. Eukaryotic cells also contain other membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum. Prokaryotes are generally smaller and structurally simpler. Examples of prokaryotic organisms include bacteria (such as E. coli) and archaea. Examples of eukaryotic organisms include humans, yeast, oak trees, and mushrooms — essentially all organisms except bacteria and archaea.
